# Electrology: practice, evidence, terminology, and client experience

Research date: **2026-11-17**. Intended use: Evergreen Electrolysis Studio, American Canyon, California, planning toward summer 2027. This is a research reference for later practitioner review and product decisions, not treatment instructions or an individual suitability assessment. No equipment settings, anesthetic regimens, or automated eligibility rules are established here.

The existing `README.md` and `Research_Sources.md` were read as project history. Their claims, pricing assumptions, and links were not treated as fresh verification. Current sources below control this report. Statutory interpretation, infection-control procedures, privacy architecture, and consent implementation also require the other research reports in this collection.

## 1. Findings that should govern future work

Electrology can provide permanent hair removal, but that does not support a promise that every follicle will be eliminated in one insertion, that every client will finish within a specified number of months, or that future hormonal changes cannot produce newly visible hair. The defensible public explanation describes the treatment, its potential benefit, repeat visits, and individual variation. It avoids giving a hypothetical client a success rate that the available evidence cannot predict.

Use **client**, **electrologist**, **consultation**, **treatment**, and **health history** in Evergreen's ordinary interface. California's electrolysis curriculum uses client terminology and explicitly includes evaluating health-history compatibility with the modalities. This supports asking relevant safety questions without pretending to diagnose a condition. It does not prescribe a particular universal intake form. [S1]

California's electrology authorization is distinct from authorization to practice medicine or surgery. Nonetheless, health relevance, a medical-device classification, and a person's reason for seeking hair removal are separate questions. “Not a physician's practice” is not a shortcut to “health information does not need protection” or “HIPAA can never apply.” [S2, S3, S13]

## 2. Evidence classification and claim boundaries

| Claim or issue | Classification and source | What it supports; limit |
|---|---|---|
| California electrolysis removes or destroys hair using an electric needle, including thermolysis | State law, BPC §7316(g), Board publication [S2] | A specific occupational scope; not permission to diagnose disease or perform every aesthetic procedure. |
| This license does not itself authorize medicine or surgery | State law, BPC §7320 [S2] | Use accurate credentials and describe the actual licensed service. |
| Needle-type epilators use AC, DC, or both and are Class I devices, exempt from premarket notification subject to limitations | Federal regulation, 21 CFR §878.5350 [S3]; FDA product classification [S4] | Device regulation is not FDA approval of Evergreen, its practitioner, or a guaranteed result. |
| Electrolysis is a permanent hair-removal method requiring a course of appointments | FDA historical consumer education [S5]; current dermatology professional education [S7] | Capability and treatment expectations; neither source establishes an individual completion date. |
| Consultation, consent, health-history updates, privacy, and referral belong in practice | Professional association standards [S8] | An operational professional benchmark, not automatically California statutory language. |
| One modality is universally best, fastest, safest, or least painful | Not established by the research reviewed | Small, old comparisons cannot settle every device, operator, body area, or client. |
| “My treatment took X hours” or “my skin healed in Y days” | Anecdote | Useful questions for research or consultation, not a denominator-based safety or effectiveness estimate. |

Regulatory sources are strongest for legal classification, not comparative effectiveness. Primary studies supply observed outcomes within their designs, not universally transferable promises. Professional associations provide useful practice norms but also promote their profession and credentials. Commercial provider pages and testimonials should not resolve disputed clinical or legal claims.

## 3. What the modalities do

Electrology generally involves a fine probe placed through the follicle's natural opening alongside the hair, with electrical energy applied to damage hair-growth structures. The public often uses “electrolysis” for the whole service; technically, galvanic electrolysis is one modality. [S6]

| Modality | Basic principle | What a future explanation should avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Galvanic | Direct current produces a chemical reaction in the follicle. | Claiming chemistry automatically makes it more permanent or universally safer. |
| Thermolysis | High-frequency alternating current generates heat. | Assuming faster energy delivery guarantees faster completion of a whole treatment course. |
| Blend | Combines galvanic and thermolysis effects. | Treating a combined mechanism as proof of superiority for every client. |
| Multiple-needle galvanic | A galvanic delivery approach using more than one probe. | Presenting it as a completely separate biological mechanism. |

The first three descriptions are supported by AEA process education and the AC/DC distinctions in the federal device regulation; AEA separately names multiple-needle galvanic in its standards. [S3, S6, S8] Selection belongs to a trained practitioner using the actual equipment's instructions and a client assessment. A website quiz should not prescribe modality or current settings.

Do not confuse professional probe electrology with products that grip a hair with electrically energized tweezers. FDA's archived consumer article distinguishes them and reports insufficient significant evidence that tweezer epilators permanently remove hair. That is a distinction about methods, not a reason to evaluate a particular modern product without its own evidence. [S5]

## 4. Permanence, growth cycles, and planning a treatment course

Several sessions are ordinarily needed because hair grows in cycles. Electrolysis is not dependent on targeting dark pigment, and can address light-colored hair. [S7] This helps explain its usefulness without falsely suggesting equal speed or identical risk across every hair, skin type, and treatment area.

For Evergreen, distinguish three observations in consultation and documentation:

1. **Visible clearance:** hair visible at a particular visit has been removed.
2. **Course progress:** the amount or distribution of unwanted growth is changing over repeat visits.
3. **Long-term outcome:** the agreed treatment goal remains met over an appropriate observation period.

These are proposed reporting distinctions, not new clinical endpoints. A photograph immediately after treatment can demonstrate visible clearance while telling us little about long-term regrowth. Repeating such a photograph in marketing as proof of permanent clearance would overstate what it measures.

AEA lists hair characteristics, cycles, past removal, heredity, hormonal influences, and medications among variables affecting the course. Its consumer page gives an approximately 18-month expectation for many people following a plan. Treat that as association guidance, **not an Evergreen guarantee or a validated prediction model**. [S6] The practitioner should give a provisional estimate only after assessment, explain assumptions, and revisit it as observations accumulate.

Newly visible hair after earlier treatment is not enough, by itself, to identify failed treatment, successful treatment of different follicles, or hormonal change. The current AEA standards specifically acknowledge that previously dormant follicles can yield new hair despite earlier treatment. [S8] An app cannot resolve that distinction from a photo or a checkbox.

The treatment goal is hair removal. Do not claim electrolysis cures PCOS or another endocrine cause of hair growth. The Endocrine Society's hirsutism guideline treats direct hair removal as an option alongside medical management, supporting a separation between the hair-removal service and assessment of an underlying cause. Its population is premenopausal women with hirsutism; it is not a universal screening or medication protocol for all Evergreen clients. [S12]

## 5. What the primary studies actually tell us

This was a targeted research review, not a systematic review or meta-analysis. Abstract-level access limits interpretation of selection, allocation, follow-up completeness, adverse events, and operator technique. The studies below demonstrate both genuine evidence and its limitations.

| Primary study | Observations verified in the source | Appropriate interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Peereboom-Wynia, 1975; 11 women with idiopathic facial hirsutism [S9] | Compared diathermy and galvanic treatment; both damaged hair roots. Differences in hair density and regrowing hair characteristics were reported. | Contrary to an absolute “no comparison studies exist” claim, a comparison exists. A small historical study does not choose today's modality for a particular client. |
| Kligman and Peters, 1984; histologic thermolysis comparison [S10] | Compared injury around follicles treated with bare versus insulated bulbous probes; reported differences in tissue-injury distribution. | Tissue mechanisms matter, but histology is not a modern comparative estimate of total clearance, pain, or long-term safety. Do not adopt its parameters as a protocol. |
| Görgü and colleagues, 2000; 12 participants, paired axillary sites [S11] | Four electrolysis sessions versus three alexandrite sessions; reported mean clearance of 35% versus 74% six months after treatment began. | Narrow, small, old comparison, with different schedules and fixed treatment sides. It is not an electrolysis lifetime failure rate, nor proof that laser is best for all hair colors and purposes. |

Each reviewed paper answers a narrower question than “what will happen to this client?” We did not identify a large contemporary comparative evidence base sufficient to produce a trustworthy universal per-session kill rate, total-hour estimator, or modality ranking. This is a limit of this search and the reviewed evidence, not proof that no other relevant work exists.

The product implication is to support documented observation and practitioner judgment. It should not convert historical percentages into automated predictions. If Evergreen later measures outcomes, record meaningful denominators, treatment area, elapsed follow-up, course exposure, and client goals; do not silently equate a satisfaction survey with permanent removal.

## 6. FDA language and marketing claims

The safest proposed public wording is: **“Electrolysis can permanently remove unwanted hair. Treatment usually requires repeat visits, and your plan depends on your hair, skin, goals, and response.”** This is a proposed synthesis for practitioner review, not mandatory wording.

Avoid **“FDA-approved studio,” “FDA-approved electrologist,”** or the sweeping **“only FDA-approved permanent hair removal.”** The current needle-epilator classification is Class I with a qualified 510(k) exemption; it does not constitute approval of a service business. [S3, S4] A particular device may have its own regulatory history, which should be verified by exact manufacturer and model before making a device-specific statement.

FDA's current cosmetics education resource links “Removing Hair Safely” to a **2019 archive capture**, where electrolysis is described as permanent hair removal. Cite it as archived FDA education, not a newly issued 2026 finding. [S5]

For comparison, an FDA-hosted laser-device submission uses “permanent reduction” for a stable long-term decrease in regrowing hair. This is a device-specific regulatory record, not a universal prohibition on every possible sentence about lasers. [S15] Avoid “laser never works permanently,” which overstates the evidence and is unnecessary to explain Evergreen's service. Modern comparative advertising would need the precise competing treatment, population, and outcome identified.

Likewise avoid “painless,” “risk-free,” “guaranteed in six sessions,” “safe for everyone,” and “zero downtime.” Do not borrow a competitor's before-and-after photographs or present stock imagery as Evergreen outcomes. An accurate small claim is more useful than an impressive claim no source can substantiate for this studio.

## 7. Discomfort, skin effects, and practitioner selection

AEA describes momentary heat or a pinch, with individual tolerance varying. It also describes temporary redness or tenderness after treatment. [S6] AAD warns of infection with unsterile probes and burns or scarring with poor technique; it notes keloid risk in darker skin and possible pain. [S7] These sources do not quantify an individualized probability or guarantee a recovery time.

Practical implications are to ask about prior skin reactions, offer a clear way to pause or stop, and establish practitioner-approved aftercare and escalation instructions. Do not normalize every reaction as expected. Conversely, do not have a generic symptom checker diagnose an infection or decide whether to continue treatment. Detailed contraindications, anesthetic safety, and infection control belong in the companion safety report and the practitioner's verified procedures.

For practitioner selection, assess evidence that can be checked:

- Current California individual and establishment licensing, with identity matching the person and premises providing service.
- Relevant experience, realistic expectations, clear fees, and a consultation that allows questions.
- Private treatment space and an understandable explanation of sterile probes, gloves, and instrument handling.
- A plan for reviewing progress, documenting reactions, and referring concerns outside the practitioner's competence.

AEA's selection page supports consultation, confidentiality, and transparent sanitation practices. [S14] Its CPE credential is expressly voluntary; it is an additional professional credential, not a substitute for the applicable state license and not evidence that the practitioner is a physician. Claim CPE, memberships, experience, or training only when verified for the actual practitioner. [S16]

## 8. Inclusive experience without unnecessary disclosure

AEA's transgender-client guidance supports correct names and pronouns, confidentiality, respect, and coordination with the surgeon's requirements when hair removal is for surgery. [S17] This does not mean every transgender client is preparing for surgery, every client wants complete hair removal, or every studio is competent to offer intimate-area or presurgical work.

Proposed Evergreen design decisions, derived from that principle:

- Ask what the client wants to achieve and which areas they want discussed. Avoid assuming goals from gender, age, or appearance.
- Allow a preferred name and optional pronouns. Collect another name only when a particular operational or legal purpose requires it, and explain that purpose.
- Ask whether voicemail, texts, and email are acceptable and what name is safe to use in those channels. Keep appointment notifications neutral.
- Make sensitive questions privately reviewable with the practitioner; do not require a detailed explanation of gender identity, trauma, or unrelated medical history to submit a first consultation request.
- Explain privacy, positioning, coverings, breaks, and any support-person policy before sensitive-area work. Scope-specific protocols need practitioner approval.
- If presurgical removal is offered, track the actual surgeon's instructions and agreed area rather than supplying a generic surgical map or a guaranteed deadline.

These are product recommendations, not a claim that every listed field is legally required. Accessibility and language needs should also be discoverable without forcing a client to disclose a diagnosis. Respectful service is an operational practice, not something established by putting an inclusive slogan on the homepage.

## 9. Health information and future build rules

HHS bases HIPAA applicability on covered-entity/business-associate definitions; a health-care provider's covered status involves specified electronic transactions. Neither choosing the word client nor collecting health history settles that analysis. [S13] California privacy obligations and contractual duties require their own review. Keep this distinction in every later technology decision.

AEA's 2026 standards call for written informed consent, the ability to stop, ongoing health-history assessment, secure records, and separate permissions for documentation versus marketing images. [S8] Future implementation should preserve an auditable practitioner review, the consent version shown, and subsequent changes. A signed web form is a record of a process, not proof that every concern was resolved.

For the summer 2027 build, the clinical boundaries are: no automated approval/rejection from medical answers; no diagnostic recommendations; no invented equipment or numbing instructions; no guaranteed treatment estimator; no collecting a full sensitive intake in a public contact form; no publishing unverified credentials or outcomes. The security and consent reports should define the actual storage, access, delivery, retention, and signing design before real client data is accepted.
