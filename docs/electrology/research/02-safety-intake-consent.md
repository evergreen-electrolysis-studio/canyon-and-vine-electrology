# Electrology safety, intake, consent, and infection prevention

Research date: **2026-11-17**. Intended setting: Evergreen Electrolysis Studio, American Canyon, California; planned opening summer 2027. This is a research and design reference, not a treatment protocol, finalized consent, or client clearance. No real client data was collected. Reverify sources before adopting procedures or launching forms.

## Findings that should govern the build

Use **client** in the studio's forms. A health questionnaire can support responsible personal services without turning the business into a medical practice. The important boundary is who makes decisions: the licensed electrologist reviews suitability within scope, and an appropriate healthcare professional evaluates medical issues. Software records the process; it does not diagnose, prescribe, or approve treatment.

The central design distinction is **submitted versus reviewed**. Receiving a signed packet must never produce “medically cleared,” “safe for treatment,” or an automatic appointment approval. Several affirmative answers require discussion, equipment-specific information, or referral; a negative questionnaire still cannot replace observing the treatment area.

Do not encode internet contraindication lists as universal bans. In particular, passive dental metal, electronic implants, pregnancy, medications, and allergies are different questions. Do not advertise that a physician's letter can override the electrologist's scope, a manufacturer prohibition, or a California restriction.

One consequential distinction, also correctly identified in the existing business plan: **California's current weekly temperature check, AEA's monthly biological testing recommendation, and CDC's weekly biological testing recommendation are different controls.** They must not be represented as a single California “monthly spore-test law.”

## Current California infection-control baseline

Article 12 requires an autoclave or dry-heat sterilizer for electrolysis. Reusable sterilizable electrolysis tools require cleaning followed by compliant sterilization using manufacturer instructions. Every sterilized package needs a chemical indicator; packages remain intact until use. Used tools require labeled contaminated storage. Equipment temperature must be checked weekly. Used or compromised probes go immediately into puncture-resistant sharps containers, replaced by three-quarters full and disposed of as biohazardous waste. Hands must be cleaned before each client. Services cannot be performed on inflamed, broken, infected, or eruptive skin. Section 984 expressly distinguishes bloodborne conditions such as HIV/AIDS and hepatitis B from its communicable-disease exclusion. These are selected requirements, not the full inspection checklist. [S1: §§978, 982–984]

For other tools and surfaces, consult §§977–981 and 990 directly; do not substitute a disinfecting wipe for required instrument sterilization. [S1]

The April 2023 proposed revision contained monthly independent-laboratory spore testing and one-year results retention. The August 17, 2026 Board materials say the health-and-safety proposals are on hold pending Sunset updates; the November 16 materials confirm that they remain on hold pending implementation of SB 1363. Current §982 still has the weekly temperature language. **The proposed monthly requirement is not the adopted rule verified here.** Check the rulemaking page again before opening. [S2–S3]

## Screening: uncertainty and review table

AEA calls for reassessment each visit, postponement when contraindications are suspected, and referral when indicated. Its implant guidance seeks written physician approval informed by modality and treatment area. This is not a universal clearance algorithm. [S4: §7]

The table below is a **proposed information-routing design**, not a clinical decision table. “Hold” means the system leaves the matter unresolved for the practitioner; it does not assert that a condition permanently prohibits electrolysis.

| Topic | Important uncertainty | Proposed form and review behavior |
|---|---|---|
| Pacemaker, ICD, cochlear implant, neurostimulator or other electronic implant | The actual implant, epilator, modality, treatment location, and manufacturer restrictions matter. Medtronic places electrolysis in its precautions category; this is not permission for every implant. [S7] | Separate electronic implants from ordinary dental metal. Ask device type and whether details can be supplied privately. Hold treatment planning until the practitioner resolves compatibility and necessary healthcare input. Never display “thermolysis is always safe.” |
| Dental implants, braces, fillings, artificial joints, plates or pins | Metal is not one uniform risk category. Sterex's FAQ discusses braces/fillings and modality selection rather than a universal exclusion. Its UK advice is not California law. [S8] | Ask type/location and intended treatment area. Do not reject an inquiry because “braces” is checked. Avoid automated modality advice or instructions about current paths; obtain equipment-specific guidance. |
| Pregnancy or possible pregnancy | AEA lists pregnancy for assessment; Sterex offers a stricter pregnancy policy. Neither is proof of a universal California prohibition or universal safety. [S4, S8] | Offer a private-discussion option. Route to practitioner review of training, manufacturer instructions, insurer policy, and healthcare input. Do not promise that one modality is safe at every stage. |
| Diabetes, immune suppression, bleeding/healing concerns | A diagnosis label alone does not establish current status or treatment suitability. [S4] | Ask a focused question about conditions affecting healing/bleeding and relevant care instructions. Record review or referral, not an automated severity score. Do not demand an entire medical chart. |
| Medications, supplements, topical products, recent changes | Names and timing may matter; the studio cannot decide that a prescribed drug should be stopped. AEA explicitly reserves prescription-product changes to the client's provider. [S4] | Allow current names, recent changes, and discussion in person. Flag missing or changed information. Never generate medication stop/start instructions or fixed washout periods. |
| Allergies and previous reactions | Relevant exposures include products and glove materials; “sensitive skin” and a documented allergy are not interchangeable. [S8b] | Ask what caused the reaction and what happened, in the client's words. Keep suspected reactions distinguishable from confirmed information. Practitioner selects compatible supplies; software does not recommend a replacement probe or product. |
| Rash, infection, open injury, sunburn, active lesion, or unusual mole in treatment area | A questionnaire cannot examine skin; California restricts treatment on the skin conditions described above. [S1] | Flag for direct examination and possible postponement/referral. Permit clients to ask privately. Do not let a photo classifier diagnose a lesion or override a hold. |
| Recent peels, surgery, injections, resurfacing or other skin treatment | Required intervals depend on the actual procedure and healing; a single generic waiting period is unreliable. [S4] | Ask procedure, date, location and any treating-provider instructions. Let the practitioner document the relevant resolution. |
| HIV, hepatitis or other chronic diagnosis | Do not confuse standard precautions with selective exclusion. [S1] | No discriminatory automatic rejection. Ask only information justified by the practitioner's actual treatment assessment; do not collect a broad infectious-disease registry for reassurance. |
| Prior scarring, pigment changes, or poor reaction to hair removal | The American Academy of Dermatology identifies burns, scarring, infection and discomfort as relevant electrolysis risks. [S10] | Ask about previous treatment experience and concerns. Preserve skin response history for the practitioner; avoid promises based on skin tone or past success alone. |

A medical referral request, when appropriate, should describe the proposed electrolysis modality and body area and ask the relevant question. An unexplained “clearance” checkbox gives both parties less useful information. The electrologist still decides whether the service can be delivered within scope and the actual equipment's restrictions.

## Questionnaire structure for Evergreen

The following is an original proposed structure. It is deliberately smaller than a general healthcare intake. Each field needs a documented purpose before implementation.

1. **Identity and contact:** name, preferred name, one reliable contact method, and information actually needed to match the booking. Establish whether full birth date and postal address are necessary before making them mandatory. Collecting age eligibility may be enough for some workflows; retention and identity requirements need separate resolution.
2. **Treatment goals:** requested area, prior hair-removal experience, questions and expectations. Do not require a client to disclose gender identity, diagnosis, or the personal reason for hair removal to receive an ordinary consultation.
3. **Focused health review:** grouped questions from the table, with conditional details instead of a large compulsory medical-history essay. Provide “yes,” “no,” “not sure,” and “discuss privately” where appropriate. A blank or uncertain answer stays unresolved rather than becoming “no.”
4. **Recent changes:** new symptoms in the area, medications/products, procedures, devices, or prior-session reactions. This should become a short follow-up check, not repeated full intake at every visit.
5. **Preferences and accommodations:** communication, comfort, accessibility, relevant boundaries, and whether a support person is desired. Avoid unnecessary sensitive details in scheduling notifications.
6. **Acknowledgment:** the client supplied information to the best of their knowledge and will report changes. Keep this separate from informed treatment consent and from marketing permissions.

Use plain language and explain why sensitive questions are asked. Provide a paper or in-person alternative. A client who cannot complete a smartphone form should not lose access to a consultation. The practitioner must be able to document a spoken answer accurately without impersonating the client's signature.

## Consent and the client journey

AEA's practice standards describe initial and continuing health assessment, treatment documentation, appropriate referral, a written informed-consent document, questions answered, a client/guardian signature when applicable, and the ability to stop treatment. They also address confidential records and limited sensitive information in electronic communications. These are professional standards; they do not alone establish the legal sufficiency of Evergreen's eventual form. [S5: Basic Practice II, VI; Informed Consent I–II; Privacy I]

Proposed sequence:

| Stage | What the client experiences | What the practitioner/system records |
|---|---|---|
| Inquiry | A simple way to request contact; no health questionnaire in a public contact box | Contact details and request status only |
| Private invitation | An identifiable studio invitation with an expiry/reissue path | Invitation identity and status; no sensitive answers in the URL |
| Intake | Accessible questions, save/correction path, explanation of review | Submitted version and unresolved responses |
| Consultation | Conversation, skin observation, realistic expectations and questions | Practitioner review, relevant flags, decisions and any referral; unresolved matters remain visible |
| Consent | Clear proposed service, material risks, expected responses, alternatives including deferral, and right to stop | Exact document version, signer, date/time, discussion acknowledgment and any limitations |
| Treatment | Continued communication; permission can be withdrawn | Session note and response, written by the practitioner |
| Aftercare | Understandable instructions and how to report a concern | Version given, relevant adaptations, follow-up responsibility |
| Return visit | A brief changes check and discussion of the last response | New information without overwriting the original history |

Do not bundle treatment consent with marketing texts, promotional photographs, or a waiver presented as eliminating professional responsibility. Distinguish clinical photographs from publication permission. Decide the minors policy before building guardian flows; an electronic signature field by itself does not resolve capacity, authority, or California consent questions.

The downloadable client copy should match what was actually signed. If wording changes, the system retains the former version and records any new signature. A database row that always points to the latest template cannot demonstrate what a previous client read.

## Infection prevention as an operational system

AEA recommends sterile single-use probes, sterilized reusable tweezers, appropriate cap processing, standard precautions, and biological monitoring with a permanent assurance file. Chemical indicators alone do not guarantee sterility. [S4: §§1–5]

| Control | Verified source distinction | Evergreen implementation proposal |
|---|---|---|
| Chemical indicator | California: each sterilized package | Log packages/cycles without claiming an indicator alone proves sterility. |
| Temperature check | California: weekly equipment check | Schedule a distinct task; do not label it a spore test. |
| Biological indicator | AEA: at least monthly per sterilizer | Baseline professional reference; review against actual equipment instructions. |
| Biological indicator | CDC healthcare guidance: at least weekly | Consider adopting the stronger interval, with practitioner/insurer input; label it studio policy, not a California mandate. |
| Actual cycle operation | Manufacturer-specific | Maintain model, instructions version, approved packaging/loading, service and validation information. No generic cycle settings in software. |

CDC recommends mechanical and chemical monitoring of each load and withholding items when indicators show inadequate processing. Its biological monitoring and failure-response guidance must be read with the actual equipment instructions. [S6: §16]

The operating procedure should map each item to its processing route: disposable, sterilizable, or appropriately disinfectable. Include the epilator controls, treatment table, magnification equipment, reusable tools, containers, and cleaning tools. Keep a physical dirty-to-clean flow and enough instrument inventory that a busy appointment never becomes an incentive to shorten processing.

Hand hygiene, glove changes and environmental cleaning need a workflow that survives ordinary interruptions. For example, answering the phone, opening a drawer, handling a personal device, and returning to the client must have a defined safe sequence. Gloves are not a substitute for clean hands. Select PPE for the actual task, including instrument cleaning and splash/chemical exposure; maintain product labels and safety information. Cal/OSHA's bloodborne-pathogen rule covers exposure assessment, controls, training, vaccination and post-exposure arrangements when applicable. [S9: (c)–(g)]

Before opening, choose a compatible sterilizer, instrument/packaging system, biological-testing service, and commercial sharps-disposal route. A household sharps program should not be assumed to accept business waste. The location-specific waste and employee compliance decisions belong on the launch checklist, not hidden inside the client form project.

## Aftercare, adverse reactions, and escalation

Aftercare should be a practitioner-approved document linked to the actual service and products used. Separate expected transient effects from problems requiring follow-up; do not promise a universal recovery time or say that all scabbing proves either normal healing or poor technique. Sterex distinguishes adverse reactions that warrant stopping/reassessment from occasional healing responses. AAD identifies infection, burns and scars as possible problems. [S8b, S10]

Proposed incident workflow: stop the service if an unexpected reaction occurs; record factual observations, timing, products and client-reported symptoms; provide only advice within scope; arrange appropriate medical evaluation when indicated; document contacts and follow-up. Preserve the original note and add dated corrections. Do not diagnose an infection or allergy from a client photograph.

General wound guidance identifies increasing redness/pain, pus, fever and related deterioration as reasons to contact a medical professional. These signs should not be dismissed by an automated “normal aftercare” reply. Breathing difficulty, collapse or other severe acute symptoms require emergency response rather than waiting for studio email. [S12; S11]

Ask whether numbing products were used. FDA has warned about high-concentration topical anesthetic products and serious systemic effects, including breathing problems, seizures and irregular heartbeat; absorption risks increase with certain application practices. The site must not recommend internet “numbing hacks,” dose schedules, occlusion, or stopping prescribed medicine. [S11]

For a sharps/body-fluid exposure, the plan should provide immediate first aid and prompt professional medical evaluation, with the relevant contacts available before an incident occurs. Do not wait for the client's disease history or a form response to decide whether to obtain help. If employees are covered, §5193 requires immediate availability of confidential medical evaluation and follow-up after a reported exposure. [S13; S9(f)(3)]

## Records, retention, and software consequences

Keep four concepts distinct: client history, consent evidence, session notes, and operational safety logs. They serve different purposes and may have different access and retention requirements. **This research did not verify a universal California electrologist client-record retention number.** That is an unresolved requirement, not proof that there is no obligation. Resolve it using current law, insurer terms, minors policy, foreseeable claims and privacy requirements before implementing deletion.

Do not import HIPAA's six-year documentation provision as a universal six-year treatment-record rule. Do not import the proposed one-year sterilizer-results period into client records. AEA's permanent assurance-file recommendation concerns sterilization reports and is not a client-record retention schedule. A documented legal hold must prevent routine deletion when appropriate.

For a custom system, the minimum useful safety design is a private practitioner review screen, a queue of unresolved matters, versioned documents, restricted access, audit history, secure client copies, backups with tested restoration, and a paper fallback. Detailed privacy/security/legal architecture is covered by the companion research; email notifications should say a packet is ready rather than include health answers.

Proposed states are **draft → submitted → practitioner reviewed → follow-up needed or ready for the next agreed step**. Treatment consent and appointment status remain separate. No AI eligibility scoring, image diagnosis, automatic treatment parameters, or automatic medical advice is needed for this business. Test the workflow with fabricated clients, including uncertainty, changed medication, incomplete signature, withdrawn consent, expired invitation, guardian ambiguity and an adverse-reaction report.

Before production, the licensed practitioner should approve every health question, response pathway, consent paragraph and aftercare instruction against training and actual equipment. The owner should resolve insurer and legal requirements, records retention, emergency/exposure arrangements, and staffing applicability. These are specific unresolved design inputs; none prevents creating and testing an empty, private prototype now.
